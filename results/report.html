Biomarkers of ASD

PSTAT 197A - Module 1 Biomarker Analysis

Author

Updated

Abstract

We use serum protein measurements to explore how analysis choices affect the search for potential biomarkers of autism spectrum disorder (ASD). Our four questions examine log transformation, outliers, changes to protein selection, and alternative classifiers. We consider whether selecting proteins using training data alone, choosing larger panels, or combining rankings through a fuzzy intersection changes the analysis. In the alternative-classifier comparison, LASSO had the highest observed cross-validated accuracy, while a smaller panel reduced the number of proteins at some cost to prediction. The project examines both predictive performance and the stability of the selected panels.

Dataset

The publicly available data from Hewitson et al. (2021) contain serum measurements from 154 boys: 76 with ASD and 78 typically developing (TD). The SOMAScan assay measured 1,317 protein variables. The dataset also records group labels and Autism Diagnostic Observation Schedule (ADOS) scores, which are excluded from our classifiers.

Our analysis retains all 1,317 proteins, including the 192 excluded from the published study. The shared preprocessing script takes base-10 logarithms, centers and scales each protein, and clips standardized values to the range -3 to 3. We examine the effects of logging and clipping separately. In Q4 cross-validation, preprocessing parameters and protein selection are determined from training data before evaluating held-out subjects.

Summary of published analysis

Hewitson et al. (2021) combined protein rankings from random forests, group t-tests, and correlations with ADOS scores. Proteins shared by the top-ten lists formed a core panel, and additional candidates were considered for improving prediction. Logistic regression was used to classify ASD and TD subjects, with repeated training/test splits used to evaluate performance.

As described in the README, our simplified in-class procedure retains 192 variables excluded from the original study and combines t-test and random forest selections. It omits repeated forest fitting, forward selection in logistic regression, and repeated AUROC estimation. The in-class results are therefore not expected to match the original study. We use this implementation as the starting point for the team’s four questions.

Improved classifier

Question 4: Alternative classifier

We asked whether a different classifier could improve prediction or use fewer proteins. The validated in-class baseline combines adjusted Welch t-tests and random forest selection with logistic regression. We compared it with LASSO, which shrinks coefficients and selects proteins, and logistic regression using a small panel of one to three highly ranked proteins. Inner five-fold cross-validation chooses the LASSO penalty and small-panel size, favoring simpler models when prediction errors are similar.

All three procedures use the same five repeats of stratified five-fold cross-validation, with preprocessing and selection performed within training folds. Accuracy, AUROC, sensitivity, and specificity are averaged over the repeats; protein counts summarize the 25 outer training fits. ASD is the positive class, with predicted probabilities above 0.5 classified as ASD.

The historical in-class model used DERM, RELT, FSTL1, and IgD and achieved 83.9% accuracy. Because preprocessing and protein selection used all subjects before its 80/20 split, this result is a separate reference and is not directly comparable with the cross-validated estimates.

In Table 1, LASSO had the highest observed validated accuracy (71.6%) and AUROC (0.793), using an average of 16.36 proteins. Its accuracy was 3.2 percentage points above the validated baseline (68.3%). The small-panel procedure achieved 64.2% accuracy using just 1 to 2 proteins per fit (mean 1.12). Its 4.2-point loss was within the prespecified five-point descriptive margin, although its AUROC and sensitivity were also lower.

The final small-panel fit selected DERM (dermatopontin; Table 2). The negative coefficient links higher standardized DERM levels with lower modeled odds of ASD. DERM was selected in 68.0% of outer training fits, the highest frequency shown in Figure 1. Selection still varied across partitions, and cross-validation evaluated the selection procedure rather than a fixed DERM-only panel.

The accuracy differences in Figure 2 vary across repeats. LASSO’s observed gain does not establish better population performance, and the small panel’s reduced size does not establish equivalent accuracy. These comparisons remain exploratory because they use many candidate proteins and repeatedly evaluate the same 154 subjects. The fixed DERM panel has not been independently validated.

Conclusion

This project examines how preprocessing and protein selection shape the search for ASD biomarkers. Comparing transformations, outliers, training-only selection, larger panels, and fuzzy intersections provides a way to assess the choices behind the original analysis. Among the alternative classifiers, LASSO offered the strongest observed prediction, while the small panel offered greater simplicity at some loss of accuracy. The limited sample and variation in selected proteins mean that these results are candidates for further study, rather than evidence of a clinically useful biomarker panel.
